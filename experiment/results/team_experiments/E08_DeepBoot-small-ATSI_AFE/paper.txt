DeepBoot: Dynamic Scheduling System for Training
and Inference Deep Learning Tasks in GPU Cluster
Zhenqian Chen , Xinkui Zhao , Chen Zhi , and Jianwei Yin
Abstract —Deep learning tasks (DLT) include training and infer-
ence tasks, where training DLTs have requirements on minimizing
average job completion time (JCT) and inference tasks need sufﬁ-
cient GPUs to meet real-time performance. Unfortunately, existing
work separately deploys multi-tenant training and inference GPU
cluster, leading to the high JCT of training DLTs with limited GPUs
when the inference cluster is under insufﬁcient GPU utilization due
to the periodic inference workload. DeepBoot solves the challenges
by utilizing idle GPUs in the inference cluster for the training
DLTs. Speciﬁcally, 1) DeepBoot designs adaptive task scaling (ATS)
algorithm to allocate GPUs in the training and inference clusters
for training DLTs and minimize the performance loss when re-
claiming inference GPUs. 2) DeepBoot implements auto-fast elastic
(AFE) training based on Pollux to reduce the restart overhead by
inference GPU reclaiming. Our implementation on the testbed and
large-scale simulation in Microsoft deep learning workload shows
that DeepBoot can achieve 32% and 38% average JCT reduction
respectively compared with the scheduler without utilizing idle
GPUs in the inference cluster.
Index Terms —Deep learning system, distributed training, elastic
deep learning, GPU cluster scheduling.

Therefore, we can focus on designing a scheduling mecha-

namic allocation of training resources. The existing multi-tenant
training system [9],[14] can dynamically adapt the number
of GPU in intervals (such as 60 seconds in Pollux [14] and
Chronus [28]). However, challenges arise when directly apply-
ing these schemes in the training-inference system.
First, allocating DLTs in the GPU cluster is an NP-hard
problem since different GPU numbers and distributions can
seriously affect the training performance. Pollux [14] takes a
genetic algorithm NSGA II [29] to approximately search for
the best solution on small scale. However, when combining
training and inference GPU clusters, the number of workers and
tasks dramatically increases, leading to the large solution space
searching. In this case, the genetic algorithm cannot ﬁnd the
optimal solution effectively with a limited population size. Be-
sides, the high complexity of NSGA II leads to a non-negligible
scheduling overhead for upcoming inference tasks, which limits
the larger population size setting.
Second, how and when to loan and reclaim the GPUs in the
inference cluster should be taken into consideration. Improper
GPU selection for reclaiming can leads to a large efﬁciency loss
for training DLTs. Besides, an aggressive loaning strategy such
as utilizing all idle inference GPUs for training DLTs can lead
to high restart overhead when the conservative strategy cannot
sufﬁciently utilize the idle resources.
Third, the distributed training allocation is updated using the
default “Stop-Resume” strategy. However, this results in high
overheads for checkpoints, datasets and container lifecycles.
Elastic training [30],[31],[32] can effectively reduce these high
restart overheads caused by inference preemption. Current elas-
tic training framework only record the dataset access progress
when the allocation changes and default model weights are
saved at speciﬁed iteration rounds. Pollux uses “Stop-Resume
by default and saves model weights only when DLT allocation
changes. This has an advantage that users do not need to cus-
tomize weight-saving iteration rounds but conﬂicts with existing
elastic training work on weight management.

dition, it manages the lifecycle of a task in training-inference
system Section IV. Meanwhile, to cope with the high restart
overhead of training DLTs caused by inference task reclaiming,
we propose auto-fast elastic (AFE) based on Pollux to achieve

B. Management of DL Cluster
Inference service GPU cluster faces the problem of low
GPU utilization due to the diurnal periodicity of inference
request [19],[23]. Since the deployment of inference service is
usually one(GPU)-to-one(service) or one-to-many by inference
scheduling framework [17],[25],[38], a large amount of GPUs
are left unused in low workload. Although distributed inference
can fully utilize the multi-GPUs with model or data parallel, it
is not discussed in this paper since it is usually applied for large
model [18] or cloud-edge inference collaboration [39],[40].
Multi-tenant training GPU cluster has a similar workload
distribution [20],[21],[22]. Nevertheless, the main bottleneck
of training DLTs is insufﬁcient GPU allocation instead when
allocation scheme is ideal [14]. The more GPUs means training
DLTs can increase the distributed training scale (for example,
from single worker one-GPU to single worker multi-GPUs).
The elasticity of training DLTs makes it possible to utilize idle
GPUs in inference cluster. Unfortunately, when to reclaim the
idle GPUs signiﬁcantly affects the efﬁciency of training DLTs.

inference GPU and selection scheme. Speciﬁcally, we reserve
the idle GPUs for inference tasks if the workload is busy and the

In addition, the placement of inference tasks is not an NP-hard
problem since the inference service occupies one GPU. In the
training-inference system, when allocating inference tasks to
idle GPUs, we should guarantee that the remaining resources
can maximize the performance for distributed training. If the
idle GPUs are used up, we can minimize the reduction of
metrics, such as GOODPUT [14] orTHROUGHPUT [12] of train-

potential resource waste. The detailed process is introduced in

C. Existing Scheduling Algorithm
Training DLT can adjust the task scale by setting the number
of GPUs. Existing DL cluster schedulers, such as SOTA work
Pollux [14], can adapt the allocation of DLTs according to the
load of the system and model characteristics. Since allocation for
training DLTs is an NP-hard problem, Pollux takes the genetic
algorithm NSGA II [29] to ﬁnd the best allocation for each DLT.
Its complexity is O(kpn2m2), where k= 100 is the number of
offspring mating, pis the number of optimization objectives
(In Pollux p=2 includes SPEEDUP ratio and fairness), nis the
number of cluster jobs, and mis the number of workers in the
Pollux solution process.
Pollux limits the number of workers because larger nleads
to the larger solution space search and optimize results in this
situation can be inaccurate under limited k. Meanwhile, an
interval schedule is not available for inference service, which
further exacerbates the scheduling problem. The introduction of
inference DLTs makes the number of jobs ngreatly increase.
As shown in Fig. 3, with the increase of nandp, the scheduling
cost is too high to apply in the training-inference system.
In Pollux, the optimized target is to maximize the sum
SPEEDUP of all jobs and fair utilization to set the global optimal
placement scheme. Although it is an NP-hard problem, we can
replace the genetic algorithm with a heuristic strategy to approx-
imately satisfy the optimal goal. Optimus [12] takes a greedy
algorithm to value the beneﬁt when allocating a GPU for the
training DLT. If the addition does not accelerate the THROUGHPUT
or the number of GPUs reaches the max GPUs set for this
job, allocation will be stopped. Unfortunately, THROUGHPUT is
Fig. 3. Schedul cost of NSGA II with the change of workers and tasks.
not linear growth. For instance, distributed training in single-
worker-multi-GPUs training can be faster than multi-workers-
multi-GPUs if the bandwidth of network is low. In this case, in
a cluster with 4 U GPU workers, a job taking 4 GPUs in one
worker can be faster than the one with 5 GPUs in two workers.
When the number of GPUs continues to grow, a job with 8 GPUs
in two 4 U workers can be faster than one with 4 GPUs in one
worker.
To ﬁnd the trade-off between efﬁciency and accuracy in
scheduling, Aryl [23] models the scheduling problem as a
knapsack problem to maximize weighted max-running-time.
For each job, Aryl stores the number of GPUs as weight and
weighted execution time in corresponding GPUs as value. Fi-
nally, it takes the group knapsack algorithm to ﬁnd the max value
with its allocation. But this strategy is not friendly for the small
DLTs due to its short execution time and insufﬁcient allocation
for these tasks will lead to a bottleneck in the cluster.
Therefore, it is available to abstract the scheduling problem
as a knapsack algorithm like Aryl with a better optimization
target, such as SPEEDUP in Pollux [14]. This metric balances
the efﬁciency and throughput, which is fairer to DLTs with
different scales. With the low complexity knapsack algorithm,
the allocation result can be more stable on large scale and we
can overcome the scheduling overhead in the training-inference
system. In Section IVwe show the details of how we model the
scheduling process as a knapsack problem while maintaining
training efﬁciency.

replicas or remaining replicas after scaling down can continue
with distributed training by synchronizing their access progress
to the data set. In general, this checkpoint saving approach
incurs overhead only in terms of data synchronization and can
theoretically be achieved at a millisecond level.

larger model [18], the cost of saving checkpoints cannot be
ignored. Saving at low iteration will increase training overhead,

model weights during elastic training phase, leading to wasted
iterations and reduced efﬁciency. CheckFreq [41] discusses this
problem, but this checkpoint management is not compatible with
the management in Pollux.

III. D ESIGN
DeepBoot is a DLT scheduler based on Kubernetes [7],[42],
which implements the management of lifecycle for training and
inference DLTs. Fig. 5illustrates the system architecture.
We use SPEEDUP [14] to measure the efﬁciency of train-
ing DLTs and use periodic interval scheduling with adaptive
task scaling (ATS-T). ATS-T models the scheduling strategy
as a knapsack problem to maximize the SPEEDUP for each
job. Resources are allocated and updated for training tasks not
completed at the speciﬁed period 1⃝. The scheduler will allocate
the idle GPUs for DLTs to adjust their scale 2⃝, where the GPUs
in the training cluster will be assigned ﬁrst and then the ones in

We synchronize the state information of replicas in train-

To meet the real-time requirements of the inference task, the
controller immediately responds to the request to the inference
scheduler a⃝with adaptive task scaling inference (ATS-I). For
the GPUs in the inference cluster, the scheduler assigns the
inference task to the GPU in the PROTECT state reserved for the
inference task ﬁrst. Otherwise, the scheduler places the inference
task on the FREE GPU. When both PROTECT andFREE GPUs
are used up, the inference task reclaims the GPUs occupied by
training DLTs b⃝. Finally, the state of the inference task in the
cluster is updated to controller c⃝, where the state of the GPU in
the inference cluster includes FREE ,PROTECT , and RUN phases

In the scenario of this article, we ensure that each inference
model can be run on a single inference GPU. For the large model
needed distributed inference support, ATS-I can be invoked
multiple times to schedule or reclaim a speciﬁed number of
GPUs to meet the requirements.

3 GPUs) or [2,2,2] (3 workers with 2 GPUs) instead of only a
number. Therefore, we make an optimal speedup assumption,
where all the GPUs under the current number will be allocated
to as few workers as possible. If GPUs in one worker are used
up, we ﬁnd the next worker to allocate. The example of a job
with 6 GPUs will be allocated with [4,2] in 4 U GPU worker
(Algorithm 2line 25).
In addition, we follow Pollux’s penalty mechanism for jobs
that frequently restart and introduce penalty factors related to
the execution time and the number of restarts (Algorithm 2, line
27). In this way, training DLTs can avoid unnecessary allocation
update if the new allocation beneﬁts enough.
However, results got by the knapsack algorithm cannot be
directly applied in allocation and we need to transform the
number into GPUs on each worker. The principle of allocation
is to prioritize the unchanging allocation and leave priority to
place job replicas on workers with the least free GPUs to satisfy
the requirements (Algorithm 2, function Replica2Allocation)
shown in Fig. 6.
The complexity of group knapsack is capped at O(nm2),
where nis the number of jobs and mis the total number of
GPUs. Meanwhile, the third layer loop of group knapsack falls
far short of m(Algorithm 1Line 7) and the execution speed is
dramatically improved compared to NSGA II with O(kpn2m2)
complexity.

VI. I MPLEMENTATION
Scheduling: The DeepBoot scheduler is implemented based
on Kubernetes [7]with algorithm ATS (Algorithms 2and3).
For training DLTs, scheduling is performed at an interval of
60 seconds to update the allocation and the hyperparameters
related to GOODPUT for the current conﬁguration case. Requests
for inference GPU usage are responded to immediately upon
arrival. When the inference task reclaims the GPUs loaned by
the training DLT, there may be a problem that the training DLT
is executed too short under the current conﬁguration, leading to
the inaccurate collected throughput. Therefore, hyperparameters
related to GOODPUT do not update when GPUs are reclaimed.In our experiments, we treat the inference request as a re-
claiming event. We also do not support the execution of multiple
training and inference tasks on one GPU. It avoids the training
DLTs being less efﬁcient and guarantees the execution latency
of inference tasks.
Elastic Training: At the system level, the controller is respon-
sible for the synchronization of the Pods’ state corresponding to
each job in the AFE module and the management of the lifecycle.
We use Redis [43]to record the state of the Pods corresponding to
the training DLT. The controller updates the job state to the Redis
server after scaling up and down. The introduction of AFE results
in Pods under one training DLT may be in different states. To
achieve consistency, the Pods of one training DLT communicate
directly with the Redis-server instead of the controller, which
conﬁrms each other’s execution state to achieve synchronization
and reduces the load request handled by the controller.
At the framework level, we implement AFE on Pytorch [5]
with all-reduce. Meanwhile, the elastic training state and metric
follow the standard of PolluxAgent [37]. We mainly modify the
dataloader in the Pytorch so that it can respond to the information
passed by the controller.

Comparsion of DL Scheduler: We show the average JCT of
different scheduling algorithms in different workload scales in
Fig. 9. Compared with Optimus, Tiresias, and AFS, DeepBoot
improves efﬁciency in (2.0x, 1.1x, 1.1x), (2.0x, 1.5x, 1.3x),
and (1.9x, 2.1x, 1.4x) in small, middle and large workload
scales respectively. Optimus takes the reduction of the remaining
completion time of the current training DLTs with the addition of
a new GPU as the priority to allocate the GPU. It leads to a higher
priority of assigning more GPUs to tasks with longer execution
times and possibly fewer GPUs to smaller jobs. In addition,
Optimus limits the max replicas for each training DLTs, leading
to low GPU utilization when the cluster is idle. Tiresias does
not support elastic scaling, and jobs will be suspended when
there are not enough GPUs left to satisfy the current job. This
results in jobs that require a large number of GPU tasks, such
as ImageNet and Bert, potentially hanging due to insufﬁcient
GPUs after being reclaimed away by the inference task, and also
leaves the cluster GPU resources underutilized. The heuristic
algorithm AFS uses the strategy of allocating GPUs as much
as possible, lifting the limitation on the maximum number of
GPUs for a job. It uses the incremental ratio of throughput as
the basis for scheduling and assigns GPUs to the jobs with the
largest incremental ratio of throughput in priority. Therefore, it
outperforms Optimus based on throughput modeling in terms
of results. However, increasing the throughput can lead to a de-
crease in statistical efﬁciency due to unnecessary traversals [51].
We also compare it with Aryl’s training scheduling algorithm,
which predicts the maximum required runtime for a job and uses
the weighted maximum runtime as a factor based on the number
of GPUs as the value of the backpack problem. This orientation
causes the GPUs to be assigned preferentially to large tasks with
long execution times like Optimus. As a result, the average JCT
of Aryl is 6.4x larger than DeepBoot in the middle workload.
Both Pollux and DeepBoot aim to ﬁnd the maximum SPEEDUP
in the cluster, while Pollux allocates directly based on the
worker-GPU matrix, which can fully enumerate the solution
space and takes fairness of allocation into consideration. There-
fore, Pollux can achieve accurate modeling slightly faster than
DeepBoot at small and middle workload scales. DeepBoot uses
the knapsack algorithm to approximately get the allocation in
Pollux. Under the large scale workload, the increase in solution
space makes Pollux unable to obtain the optimal solution in a
limited number of mating rounds, and thus DeepBoot is slightly
faster than Pollux.

VIII. R ELATED WORK
GPU Cluster Scheduling: Optimus [12] models the through-
put of distributed training for PS architectures and prioritizes
GPU allocation to the tasks with the highest drop in training
time. Tiresias [10] only runs when the number of GPUs meets the
training task requirements and polls according to the time slice.
It does not support elastic training, which makes it easy for the
job to be pended when it cannot get enough GPUs and affects the
training efﬁciency. AFS [9]allocates GPUs as much as possible
and prioritizes GPUs to those with the highest percentage of
throughput. This strategy leads to frequent reclaiming and the
system needs to be supported by CoDDL to reduce the cost when
allocation updates. Aryl [23] takes the weighted runtime [54] re-
duction as the optimization objective of the knapsack algorithm
for the scheduling, which is not effective for workload with a
large number of short jobs. DeepBoot’s ATS-T approximates

user does not need to manually set the saving iteration interval.
IX. C ONCLUSION
We propose DeepBoot supporting training DLTs to utilize the
idle GPUs in the inference cluster and overcome the imbalance
GPU utilization due to the periodic difference of training and
inference workload. It manages training and inference tasks in
the cluster through the ATS scheduling algorithm, while taking
AFE to avoid the high preemption overhead of training DLTs.
ATS algorithm can support large-scale scheduling, manage the
application of inference GPUs and minimize the inﬂuence of
training DLTs. We also analyze other DL schedulers in the
training-inference situation and the results in the simulator and
testbed show our advanced performance with a reduction of
(2.0x, 1.5x, 1.3x) and (1.1x, 1.4x, 1.1x) compared with Opti-
mus, Tiresias, and AFS respectively in average JCT. Besides,
compared with the baseline of Pollux without utilizing idle
inference GPU, DeepBoot can get 32% and 38% reduction on
the testbed and simulator in average JCT. In the future, support
for heterogeneous GPUs between training and inference clusters
will be introduced in our cluster.
